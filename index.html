Reading the Clock - Turning Raw Timestamps into Forecast-Ready Data

What a time series is, why forecasting differs from regression, and how to turn a raw monthly sales sheet into clean, forecast-ready data.

Case: MobiPlusMonthly sales, Jan 2001 – Jan 2019Python · pandas
About the data The data is simulated for teaching. The numbers are not real MobiPlus figures, but they carry the same problems the real sheet had: missing months and two anomalies. Code snippets below use pandas; the series is called Sales and the date column DATE.
1 · Business case

Planning December with dirty data

You are the data scientist at MobiPlus, a mobile phone manufacturer. Before the planning meeting the factory head asks one question: "How many phones should we build for December 2018?" The only thing you have is the monthly sales sheet, loaded with pd.read_excel("mobilesales.xlsx").

Three things stand out before any model is built: months with no value (gaps in the line), one month that crashes to almost zero around 2006, and one month that spikes far above everything else around 2013. Sales also rise over time and peaks repeat; we return to those in the "Reading the plot" section.

What MobiPlus wants from the project

Goal 1 · Understand patternsKnow how demand behaves so factory maintenance and staffing can be planned around it.
Goal 2 · Hit an accuracy barA Mean Absolute Percentage Error (MAPE) of at most 5%.
Goal 3 · A range, not just a pointA range forecast next to the point forecast lets the business make trade-offs.

Why a wrong forecast costs money

A forecast lets MobiPlus plan production, hiring, purchasing, discounts, marketing and maintenance. In peak seasons the company may also run deals to promote the brand.

Over-forecastPhones pile up in the warehouse and holding cost rises. In a fast-moving phone market older models become outdated; at MobiPlus about 30% of unsold phones end up scrapped.
Under-forecastDemand goes unmet, revenue is lost and loyal customers lose goodwill. This loss is quiet, because nobody sees the sales that did not happen.

2 · Foundations

What a time series is

A time series is a signal indexed by an ordered timestamp. To qualify you need at least two things:

𝑡 · a date or timestampSeconds, days, weeks, months or years.
𝑦 · one quantity measured at that timeSales, revenue, inventory.

A signal is a series of numbers, ordered by the time they were recorded, that all represent the same physical quantity. Think of a heart monitor: the same quantity, recorded again and again, in order. Monthly phone sales fit exactly the same description.

NIFTY stock pricesthe pricedaily
Population growth rate of a countrythe rateyearly

Forecasting vs regression and classification

Forecasting is analysing history to predict future values. Given values up to now, 𝑥₁, 𝑥₂, …, 𝑥ₜ, we need 𝑥ₜ₊₁, 𝑥ₜ₊₂, …

Forecasting is still a supervised problem, and since it predicts real numbers it feels closer to regression than to classification. The twist: each month is lined up against the month before it.

3 · Preparing the data

Datetime index, completeness and the first plot

The date column must be a real datetime

Pandas offers many built-in tools for datetime values: slicing by year or month, resampling, shifting and clean plotting. A date stored as plain text gives none of these. The Excel file gave us datetime64 automatically. A CSV stores everything as text, so the same data read from CSV arrives with DATE as plain text (shown as str in recent pandas, object in older versions). The fix is a single call.

from_csv = pd.read_csv("mobilesales.csv")          # DATE is text
from_csv["DATE"] = pd.to_datetime(from_csv["DATE"])  # DATE is datetime64

Completeness check: do we have every month?

The data runs from January 2001 to January 2019: 18 full years plus one extra month, so the expected row count is 18*12 + 1 = 217. The sheet has 217 rows and 217 unique dates, so no month is absent from the calendar.

Two different problemsA month can be absent (the row is not there at all) or present with an empty value. The completeness check finds the first kind; the next section deals with the second.

Models assume every step is equally spaced. If February is followed directly by May, the model does not know that two months vanished and will learn the wrong thing.

full_calendar = pd.date_range(mobile_sales.DATE.min(), mobile_sales.DATE.max(), freq="MS")
absent = full_calendar.difference(pd.DatetimeIndex(broken.DATE))

pd.date_range with freq="MS" builds the full month-start calendar, and difference reveals exactly which dates are absent. It is a one-line audit worth running on every new dataset.

Use the dates as the index

Dates are unique, and a datetime index unlocks slicing by date text: mobile_sales.loc["2018-09":"2019-01"] just works, plots label the x-axis with real dates automatically, and later steps such as rolling() and resample() expect time to be the index. With the default 0, 1, 2 index you would do extra work for the same result.

mobile_sales = mobile_sales.set_index("DATE")
mobile_sales.Sales.plot(title="Monthly sales with a datetime index")

The gaps, the dip and the spike from the opening are all still visible, now on a proper time axis. Which to fix first? Either order can be justified. Ours is gaps first, then dip and spike, because filling the gaps gives a complete series on which the percentile cut-offs for the anomalies can be computed cleanly.

4 · Cleaning

Missing values

The completeness check passed, yet the plot still shows gaps. Those are months that exist in the calendar but carry no sales value. The plan: find where values are missing, check whether the gaps follow a pattern, and fill them with the most appropriate value.

Where are they, and is there a pattern?

If all gaps fell on the same calendar month, for example every December, that would be a story worth chasing. The grid's bottom row counts the gaps per calendar month.

Visualizer · gap map
value presentvalue missing

19 months are missing, scattered across the calendar. April has the most with 4 of the 19, and no month dominates. There is no visible pattern, so a standard fill is safe. (A pattern would have been handled differently.)

Why not simply drop the rows?

Dropping a row removes its date too, so the series is no longer equally spaced. Look at the gap between consecutive remaining months.

Visualizer · spacing after dropping
Months between each pair of consecutive surviving rows. A value of 1 is a normal step.

The 2s, 3s and the single 4 are places where a model would see up to four months as "one step". Dropping creates the irregular spacing we just warned about, so we fill instead.

Four ways to fill, compared

Mean or median

The simplest idea is one number for the whole series, like guessing a missing exam score with the class average. The mean is 10,854.2 and the median 10,832.0, so either gives a similar result. The problem: in 2002 sales were roughly 6,000 to 7,500, yet the 18-year mean sits near 10,900, so the filled line spikes up for three months and then crashes back. A phone line does not jump by 4,000 units and fall back in a quarter. In the middle years the mean happens to look natural, but only because that stretch sits near the overall average; later, once sales have outgrown the mean, the filled points sit too low.

Zero

Zero is valid in some businesses: for daily store data, a closed Sunday really is zero sales, and in industry you confirm this with the client who owns the data. For monthly phone sales a zero would imply the company sold nothing.

Linear interpolation

Fill each gap using its own neighbours. For a single missing month, take the average of the month before and after. For a longer gap, join the last known point before it and the first known point after it with a straight line. If you were at kilometre 10 at 9 am and kilometre 16 at noon, a fair guess for 10:30 am is kilometre 11: you use nearby readings, not your average position across the year.

Visualizer · fill explorer
One gap, three fills
Illustrative neighbours (defaults sit in the 2002 range of roughly 6,000 to 7,500). The global mean is the lesson's 10,854.2.

filled_mean   = mobile_sales.Sales.fillna(mean_val)
filled_zero   = mobile_sales.Sales.fillna(0)
filled_linear = mobile_sales.Sales.interpolate(method="linear")

# a single gap (April 2007) is exactly the average of its two neighbours
(march_2007 + may_2007) / 2          # 9436.0
filled_linear["2007-04-01"]          # 9436.0

Grade the fills: hide and recover

Plots are a good first check, but we can also score the fills. In a real project the true values of missing months are unknown, so use a hide-and-recover test: hide 19 months that we do know from a calm stretch of history (2007 to 2012), fill them with each method, and measure how far each fill lands from the real value.

Visualizer · scoreboard
Ranking by average miss per hidden month (lower is better). The notebook reports exact figures; this view shows the order and the stated relationships only.
    test_series[hidden_months] = np.nan
fills = {"mean": ..., "median": ..., "zero": ..., "linear interpolation": ...}
scoreboard = pd.Series({name: (s[hidden_months] - real_values).abs().mean()
                        for name, s in fills.items()})

    Linear interpolation lands closest, missing by roughly half as much as the mean and median, and zero is by far the worst. This is a habit worth keeping: when you cannot see the truth, hide part of what you do know and test your method on it.

    Looking aheadInterpolation with one neighbour on each side is the simplest case of a centered moving average. In Class 2 we widen the window and see how the same idea becomes smoothing and trend estimation.

    The interpolated series becomes the working copy: mobile_sales["Sales"] = mobile_sales.Sales.interpolate(method="linear"), leaving 0 missing values.

    5 · Cleaning

    Anomalies

    An anomaly, or outlier, is an unusual data point that stands out from the rest. It is either a wrong entry (for example, a month a data pipeline filled with a near-zero value) or a correct entry caused by a one-time event that will not repeat. Even a valid one-time event should usually be removed from the training data: if the model learns from an event that will not happen again, it becomes biased.

    Finding them

    Start with the numbers. The minimum is 108 while the 25th percentile is around 8,800, and the maximum is far above the 75th percentile.

    Lowest three
    2006-02108
    2001-024,670
    2003-025,892
    Highest three
    2013-0723,740
    2018-1216,921
    2017-1216,304

    A histogram tells the same story: the bulk of the months sit in one hump and a few bars are isolated at the far ends. February 2006 (108) and July 2013 (23,740) are the two clear anomalies.

    One option is a hand-picked cut: replace anything below 5,000 with 5,000 and anything above 20,000 with 20,000. It works here because the histogram has a clear gap, but with a more continuous histogram there would be no obvious place to cut.

    Quantile cut-offs

    A quantile rule does not depend on eyeballing. A common convention treats points below the 5th percentile or above the 95th as anomalies and caps them at those boundaries with clip(). The 95th percentile of 15,119.8 means only 5% of months sold more than that.

    Visualizer · clipping explorer
    Where do the cut-offs fall, and what do they touch?
    Dots are the six extreme months listed above. Pick a rule.

    low_5, high_95 = sales.quantile(0.05), sales.quantile(0.95)   # 6,650.2  and 15,119.8
low_2, high_98 = sales.quantile(0.02), sales.quantile(0.98)   # 6,082.6  and 15,692.5
clip_2_98 = sales.clip(lower=low_2, upper=high_98)

    The trade-off

    At 5th/95th, 22 points changed. The two anomalies are gone, but many of those points are genuine early lows and genuine late highs. Widening to 2nd/98th changes only 10: the two real anomalies (February 2006 and July 2013) plus eight genuine extremes at the very start and end of the history. No percentile setting is perfect: a wider cut-off protects genuine values but leaves more of the anomaly untouched, while a tighter one removes more anomalies and also flattens real peaks.

    Keep it symmetricKeep the percentiles symmetric unless there is a reason not to. If you move the upper bound, move the lower bound by the same amount.

    Another valid approach, when you are sure a specific entry is wrong, is to mark the anomalies as missing and then interpolate them. What to avoid is leaving them empty at forecasting time, because most models need every time step present. Clipping is used here to show the percentile idea; the 2nd/98th version is applied to the working copy.

    6 · Reading the plot

    Trend, repeating patterns and domain meaning

    With the series clean, look at the plot again. This connects to Goal 1 of the business case. Two questions can be answered just by looking:

    • Is there a trend? Yes, sales rise year after year, so the brand is earning more. Average sales per year put a number on it (groupby(index.year).mean()).
    • Are there repeating patterns? Yes, peaks and dips come back at roughly the same time every year.

    To see the repeating pattern, compare each month with its own year's average. A ratio above 1 means the month usually sells more than the year's average; below 1, less.

    relative_to_year = full_years / full_years.groupby(full_years.index.year).transform("mean")
relative_to_year.groupby(relative_to_year.index.month).mean()

    What a repeating pattern means depends on the domain, and a good forecaster knows why it happens:

    Retail storeSpikes on weekends, a dip on Mondays.

    7 · Framing the forecast

    Forecast horizon, frequency and who consumes the forecast

    Two settings decide what kind of forecast is being asked for, and they should be agreed before any model is built.

    FrequencyHow often the series has a value: daily, weekly, monthly. Ours is monthly.
    Horizon (ℎ)How many steps into the future we forecast. ℎ = 12 on a monthly series means the next 12 months.

    Frequencies in pandas

    Visualizer · alias explorer
    pd.date_range("2019-02-01", periods=3, freq=alias) — pick an alias.

    MS means month start, which matches our data.

    Rolling up is easy, going finer is not

    A finer series can be rolled up into a coarser one: monthly sales sum into quarterly or yearly sales with resample("QS").sum() or resample("YS").sum().

    Visualizer · resampling 
    daily = monthly.asfreq("D")
len(daily)              # 6544
daily.isna().sum()      # 6328

    Almost every day is empty, because the data never recorded daily sales. A finer frequency cannot be invented from a coarser one without making assumptions, so the frequency of the data limits the frequency of the forecast.

    The future calendar

    For a 12-month horizon after January 2019, we need twelve new month-start dates: pd.date_range(start=mobile_sales.index[-1], periods=13, freq="MS")[1:] gives 12 steps from 2019-02-01 to 2020-01-01.

    Who uses the forecast?

    The right frequency and horizon depend on the person reading the forecast.

    TeamDecisionFrequency neededHorizon needed
    Factory staffingShift rostersWeekly4 to 8 weeks
    Component procurementOrder parts from suppliersMonthly3 to 4 months
    Marketing campaignsPlan festive season dealsMonthly3 to 6 months
    Finance and budgetingAnnual budgetQuarterly4 quarters

    The table is an illustration to be challenged, not a fixed truth. If procurement argues for a longer horizon because suppliers have long lead times, that is a good argument and points at the real rule:

    RuleThe horizon should be at least as long as the lead time of the decision that uses the forecast.

    8 · Putting it together

    One function that makes data forecast-ready

    Everything above can be written once and reused on any new monthly file. The order matters: parse dates, set the index, check completeness, fill the gaps, then cap the anomalies.

    Visualizer · pipeline stepper
    Full function
    def make_forecast_ready(path, freq="MS", lower_q=0.02, upper_q=0.98):
    data = pd.read_csv(path, parse_dates=["DATE"]).set_index("DATE")

    # completeness check
    full_calendar = pd.date_range(data.index.min(), data.index.max(), freq=freq)
    absent = full_calendar.difference(data.index)
    assert len(absent) == 0, f"Absent dates: {list(absent)}"

    # fill gaps first, then cap anomalies
    series = data["Sales"].interpolate(method="linear")
    series = series.clip(lower=series.quantile(lower_q), upper=series.quantile(upper_q))

    # final validation
    assert series.isna().sum() == 0
    assert series.index.is_monotonic_increasing
    return series
    Rows            : 217
Missing values  : 0
Same as our step-by-step result: True

    The function reproduces the step-by-step result exactly. Re-running it with the tighter 5th/95th percentiles changes 22 values relative to the 2nd/98th result, because every one of the 22 points that the 5/95 clip touches ends at a different value. This function is reused at the start of every later class.

    A data audit

    An audit(series) function returns a dictionary with the number of rows, first and last dates, the number of missing values and the list of calendar months that are absent.

    Solution and output on the raw sheet
    def audit(series, freq="MS"):
    full_calendar = pd.date_range(series.index.min(), series.index.max(), freq=freq)
    return {"rows": len(series),
            "first": str(series.index.min().date()),
            "last": str(series.index.max().date()),
            "missing_values": int(series.isna().sum()),
            "absent_months": list(full_calendar.difference(series.index).strftime("%Y-%m"))}
    {'rows': 217, 'first': '2001-01-01', 'last': '2019-01-01',
 'missing_values': 19, 'absent_months': []}

    217 rows, 19 missing values and no absent months. Running a function like this on every new file turns "I think the data is fine" into a checked statement.

    Recap

    Key takeaways

    1. A time series is an ordered timestamp plus one quantity. Forecasting predicts its next values from its own past, so rows can never be shuffled.
    2. Forecast-ready data needs a datetime dtype and index, a completeness check, gaps filled by linear interpolation and anomalies capped with symmetric quantile cut-offs.
    3. The mean, median and zero fills ignore the local level of the series; a hide-and-recover test is the honest way to compare fills.
    4. Frequency and horizon are agreed with the people who use the forecast before any model is built, and the horizon should cover the lead time of their decision.

    Common mistakes

    Reading a CSV and forgetting to convert the date column from text to datetime.
    Assuming the data is complete because the row count looks close to expected.
    Dropping rows with missing values instead of filling them, which breaks equal spacing.
    Filling gaps with one global number such as the mean or zero.
    Clipping with very tight percentiles and flattening genuine peaks.
    Shuffling a time series, or using a daily frequency on data only recorded monthly.
    Closing the MobiPlus storyMobiPlus now has a clean, complete, equally spaced monthly series, and the team knows which decisions the forecast will feed and how far ahead each one looks. The series still carries a clear upward trend and a repeating yearly pattern. In Class 2 we take that series apart with moving averages, trend, seasonality and decomposition, and learn to spot the points where the trend changes direction.
